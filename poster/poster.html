Cut the price,
or trust the shelf?

We taught a model to predict what a perishable will sell tomorrow at any discount. Then we discovered it would discount everything, and had to teach it to read the shelf first.

01A decision every evening, for every product

6 pm, the shelf is half fullTomorrow's discount must be set tonight, before anyone knows how much will sell.
Two ways to loseHold the price and stock may be thrown away. Cut it and margin goes on sales that would have happened anyway.
Our questionFor one product, in one store, on one day: how much sells tomorrow at each discount, and should we discount at all?

04The decisions behind the numbers

k-nearest neighbours · 25 look-alike daysFinds the 25 most similar past days and averages them. Simple, and already 5% better than the average.
Random forest · 300 trees, depth 10All 16 settings we tried beat the baseline every single week. A forgiving model.
CatBoost · small and slow on purposeBoosting over-fits in plain sight (see 06), so the best version is 500 shallow trees at a 0.03 learning rate.
Blend · 50/50 forest + CatBoostTheir mistakes are 97% correlated, so blending gains only 0.001. We keep it as a safe choice, not a clever one.
Splits are strictly in time order: every validation week is predicted by a model that has never seen it. Random splits would have flattered every model by putting near-identical days on both sides.

05The twist: a good forecaster is a reckless pricer

Ask the model "what if I discount?" and it answers: deeper is always better. At a 0.6 rate it predicts 2.3× the sales.

It learned that from clearance days, which are fewer than 1% of the data. Left alone it would discount on 98% of days.

So we put the shelf before the model. Three stock rules decide first; the model only chooses among discounts the product has actually run.

06The challenges we found most interesting

The shelf lies
On 45% of days the shelf was empty for part of the day, so recorded sales are lower than real demand. The model learns from censored numbers.
Sales without units or prices
The data is normalised: 1.2 is not 1.2 kilograms or dollars. "Value" is sales × discount rate, a proxy, so profit cannot be computed.
Managers are not random
Discounts were chosen on days managers expected demand. Part of the lift the model sees is their judgement, not the price cut. Correlation, not cause.
No counterfactual
We only ever see what sold at the price the manager chose. Whether our policy sells more or wastes less needs a real experiment.
